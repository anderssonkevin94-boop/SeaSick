# SeaSick — Game Design Document

> Living document. Every AI task (Claude Code or Coplay) should reference this for design intent.
> Status: **v1 — core concept locked 2026-08-17**

## 1. High concept
A one-handed portrait mobile game. You lead an island tribe that must sail to distant islands for resources — but your people *hate* the sea. Sail smoothly to keep their stomachs settled, gather loot, and get back before seasickness turns the crew against you. Spend what you bring back on a better village and a better ship, then sail farther.

**Reference points:** Alto's Odyssey (one-thumb flow-state core), FTL (crew at stations), Cult of the Lamb (village + roster with traits), Sea of Thieves (manned-ship fantasy, scaled way down).

## 2. Pillars
1. **Sailing feels great.** One thumb, physical, wave-riding mastery. If sailing isn't fun, nothing else matters. Everything ships after this does.
2. **Sickness is the clock — and skill winds it.** Seasickness is the mission timer, but it accelerates with rough sailing (slamming waves, erratic turns) and slows with smooth sailing. Mastery visibly extends range before any upgrade does.
3. **The crew are people, not UI.** Sickness, panic, and mutiny are shown through whole-body acting on low-poly characters: green tint, staggering, abandoning a cannon mid-fight to puke over the railing. State is always readable from the gameplay camera.
4. **The camps are worth the voyage.** Whichever islands you settle (and the roster) are the progression spine — every trip makes the tribe visibly better off.

## 3. Core gameplay loop
**Prep** (pick crew, potions, cargo target) → **Sail out** (steer smooth lines through waves, manage sail trim and crew sickness) → **Gather** at the island (simple at MVP) → **Sail back heavier** (worse handling, sicker crew, push-your-luck) → **Spend** on village buildings, ship parts, potions → repeat, farther.

Failure is soft, and it is a loss of *capability* rather than an event: a crew that gets sick enough stops being able to work the ship. You lose time, cargo-carrying trips and the ability to defend yourself — never your save, and never control of the ship.

### Losing the crew (always visible, never a cliff)
The meter only ever goes up at sea. Past ~80% they start leaving their post to heave over the rail; the episodes get longer and closer together until the rail is all they do. Nothing is announced and nothing is seized — sail changes get slower, the oars weaken, guns fall silent one at a time as their gunner walks away. **The captain always keeps the tiller, and the sail stays wherever it was last set**, so a fully broken crew leaves a ship that still steers and still makes way. You lose your options, not your ship.

## 4. Platform, player & controls
- **Platform:** mobile, portrait, one-handed. Sessions = one voyage (target 3–8 min).
- **Player role:** the captain — commands, never walks the deck. Crew executes.
- **Camera:** third-person chase, ~10–15 m behind/above the ship; close enough to read whole-body acting. Automatic brief push-ins on notable events (imminent puke, mutiny stage change). No manual camera.
- **Controls (a thumb, or two):**
  - **The wheel and the lever (2026-09-22).** A ship's steering wheel sits at the bottom centre and a telegraph lever stands beside it, to the right where a thumb falls. You turn the wheel by dragging round its hub — ±180° is hard over, rudder = angle/180 — and it **holds where you leave it**, like a real wheel; a double-tap on the hub brings her amidships. The lever **latches** anywhere from full astern to full ahead, with the bottom quarter of its travel holding the whole astern range (backing is a manoeuvre, not a way to travel) and a dead band that snaps to stop. Both work at once, keyed by touch id, so one thumb can steer while the other rings down. The wheel's angle is an *order*: `ShipMotor.Rudder` is still eased toward it, so nothing snaps. This replaced a drag-anywhere-in-the-bottom-45% absolute tiller that recentred the moment you let go, and a pair of fingernail-sized ▲/▼ buttons in the corner.
  - Keyboard at a desk is unchanged: A/D steer, W/S drive, and a held key overrides the wheel or the lever and hands control straight back on release.
  - Speed emerges from sail trim and the state of the water (**not** from heading — see the 2026-08-19 wind removal)
  - Tap crew/station = small contextual order menu (trim, hold, potion, treat)
  - Tap-and-hold (tentative) = precision/zoom view
- **Input tech:** Unity Input System, touch; pointer simulation in editor.

## 5. World & setting
Archipelago explored outward in whichever direction the player chooses — there is no fixed island it radiates from; farther islands = richer resources = longer, harder voyages. **Distance is the difficulty curve**, and it is now *physical*: sea state is regional, so the water itself gets worse the further out you go — a calm shelf close in, lively water in the middle, mountainous seas at the edge.

**The premise:** the tribe has **never sailed**. Everything they know about the sea is folklore and rumour — waves the size of mountains, creatures nobody can picture, islands with stone people and mushroom trees that contradict their idea of what is real. The player goes and finds out which parts are true. Crew who come back are **heroes**, and the stories of what they survived are the reward the village actually cares about.

**Regions are a compass as well as a ring.** Distance sets *how dangerous*; direction sets *what kind*. North is cold, south is hot, east is dry, west is open ocean with scattered islands. The compass carries the cardinals for exactly this reason — "which way am I pointing" is the same question as "what am I sailing into".

**The sea is one system, not a set of zones (2026-08-28).** There are no borders in the water. Distance sets the **ceiling** — how bad it can get — and drifting storm cells decide where under that ceiling the sea actually is, so a squall can reach the home shelf (never past *rough*) and the deep west eases and gathers between about 45 and 65 m instead of sitting pinned at its maximum. Underneath that, roughness patches a few hundred metres across drift over the water and change its **texture** rather than its size: the chop swings by half across the visible sea while the long swell rolls straight through, which is what a real patch of rough water is. Every gradient is authored in **metres of wave height and interpolated geometrically**, because the eye reads height as a ratio — 3→6 m is the same step as 30→60 — so the crossing out west builds by about 20 % per hundred metres through lively, rough, heavy, wild and mountainous rather than skipping four of them in eight hundred metres. Nothing repeats: the wander, the sets, the cells and the patches all run on periods that do not divide into each other.

**Storm waves (rebuilt 2026-08-25).** Out west the sea itself becomes the mountains — **waves in the wave field, not obstacles placed in it**. The earlier design here described `SwellDirector`/`MountainSea`: ~100 m cliffs, 30 m thick, a separate moving obstacle you survived by meeting bow-on. That system died with the Gerstner stack and is **not coming back**; a wall taller than it is wide is something that kills you, not water you ride, and the premise asks for water you ride.

What replaces it is a genuine long swell: roughly **65 m of significant wave height on 470 m rollers**, so a face is a couple of hundred metres of climbable water — **about ten boat lengths** — rather than a cliff. The trade is deliberate: at that size a face steep enough to *read* as a mountain (15–20°) is close to the physical limit before water breaks, so the wavelength is chosen to buy the slope, not the height. Because they are real waves they behave like real waves — they refract, they carry the ship's own motion, and the sea lies down as the bottom comes up near land, which is what keeps them out west without a rule saying so. The one deliberate lie is the dispersion depth (30 m against a 180 m seabed), which slows the rollers to about 17 m/s so a 7.5 m/s boat can work a face instead of being overtaken at 32.

**How you survive one is still heading.** Bow-on you climb steepest and slam; quartering you traverse the face; along the crest the water is momentarily still relative to you and you can run the ridge. Beam-on in the trough is what rolls you.

**Land (rebuilt 2026-08-23).** The ocean is the default state of the world; islands are the exception. Terrain is a pure, seeded function of world position (`SeaSick.Terrain`): fBm → continentalness mask (sparse land, `landRatio`) → terrace curve (an inspector `AnimationCurve`: stepped = plateaus and cliffs, linear = hills) → detail → an altitude blend that keeps the 0–5 m band above the waterline smooth, so **every shore is a beach ramp and cliffs only start inland**. Seabed generates under the water, so there are shallows. Chunks (128 m, 65² verts) stream around the ship in Burst jobs with LOD rings; the world is unbounded by default (`worldRadius` clamps it). The starting island sits where `worldOffset` slides the nearest land under the spawn — it carries no special rules; the player can camp there or anywhere else. Waves **shoal and die at the shore** because the ocean reads a grid of terrain heights (`RegionField` shore factor). Gameplay islands (anchoring, crew, raiders) are discovered by flood-filling the height field around the ship's start and keep the old radial `Island` API, now measured off the terrain — "which side you approach from" is a slope question (`WorldSettings.beachMaxSlope`, 0.50: at 0.70 nearly every shore was landable). The minimap draws that same flood-fill mask rather than a circle per island, because a radius cannot describe a crescent.

**HUD rule: no full-width banners, ever.** A band of colour across the middle of the world is the most immersion-breaking thing a HUD can do. `UITheme.Banner` was deleted outright. Warnings live on the instruments the player is already reading, or on the world itself.

**Art direction:** graphic adventure (Option B): clean low-poly forms, cobalt water and turquoise shallows, warm timber and cream sails, grouped foliage and cool coloured shadows. Preserve the actual ship types and sail plans. Islands use broad rocky terraces, rounded broadleaf groves and fuller conifers. Trees and resource props are individually removable assets; island coves and wooded uplands retain connected usable ground. Warm and comedic. Body language over facial animation. Sickness reads at gameplay distance: skin tint shifts toward green, posture sags, walk becomes a stagger.

**Island art style — "carved" low poly (decided 2026-09-08).** Reference boards: a temperate crag (dark dense conifers, planar cliffs, beach ring, turquoise shelf, skerries) and a tropical atoll chain (sandbanks, dense palms, lagoons). Both say the same three things and the style is built from them:
- **Silhouette first.** Every triangle goes on the outline. A canopy is ONE jagged envelope (the spruce), one ray-sampled hull (the broadleaf), a fan of folded fronds (the palm) — never a stack of primitives.
- **Faceted form, soft colour.** Faces stay flat; colour is a per-corner vertex gradient — dark underneath and toward the trunk, lit on top, shade cooled toward blue. Vertex colour, which the terrain/scenery shader already reads, so it is free on mobile.
- **Rock is planes.** Convex shards with big flat faces, sunk into the ground. Boulder, ore host, skerry, sea stack and cliff are one shard at different sizes; an ore vein is a plane the shard is scored along so the stain has vertices to live on.
- **Mass over individuals.** Trees close at 4.5–5.5 m spacing; an island is judged as a wood, not as a tree.
- **Budget (portrait phone):** scenery tree ≈200 tris within 150 m, ≈115 beyond, a card past 600 m; boulders 30–40; harvest props ≤300. Two welded scenery meshes per island swapped by distance.
- **The water says the depth.** Foam line, turquoise shelf, teal, deep — the ocean's shoal tint is half of what makes the boards read as islands.
Generators: `tools/blender/seasick_style.py` (assets, contact sheet) and `tools/blender/seasick_island_v2.py` (the island structure as a visual spec: field → paint → scatter, one rule set at three sizes). Unity mapping: `RockBreak` = the proud-rock field, `VertexColour` = paint, `IslandScenery` = scatter.
- **In Unity (same day).** The kit ships as `Resources/Flora/seasick_flora.fbx` (readable, colours in the vertices, `FloraImport` sets it) and `SceneryKit` stamps it into `IslandScenery`'s welded meshes — now one mesh pair per 96 m CELL, swapped between ~200 and ~80 tris a tree by `SceneryLod` at 220 m and dropped past the streamed terrain. Cliff shards go on sheer faces (slope > 0.85, clustered by a slow noise, tilted against the face), not only where rock broke out; the terrain paint moved to the carved palette and writes "how much rock" into vertex ALPHA so the shader stops striating grass flanks it guessed were cliffs. **Kevin, on the first Unity shots: the Blender islands looked good partly because they are flat, sleek hills, not the steep domes the terrain was making** — so the relief came down (reliefHeight 60 → 42, massif 1–5.5 → 0.8–3.0, plainRelief 26 → 18, ridgeAmount 0.85 → 0.45, rockRelief 34 → 22), trees climb to 45° (vegSlope 0.6–1.0), stone paint starts at 44° (cliffRockStart 0.72), verdancyBias 0.7 so most islands are wooded. Measured with `CostProbe` against the morning baseline: triangles p50 462k vs 466k, SetPass 111 vs 119, GPU 4.39 vs 4.34 ms — the style costs what the cones cost.
- **Second pass on Kevin's Blender-sheet feedback ("the rocks and the small ridges… make the islands smaller").** Islands shrunk by raising the land threshold rather than the mask frequency, so home stays anchored (landRatio 0.15 → 0.10, skerries 0.3 → 0.4; the nearest-land home is now an 74 m islet). Proud rock became common instead of an event (rockBias 2.2 → 1.2, thresholds 0.80/0.44 → 0.72/0.40, crag scale 140 → 100 m, rockRelief 26) and the crest shards sit on it (proud > 0.8 m, 3–8 m slabs); sheer soil faces get only small slabs sunk deep, because big tilted ones floated where the ground fell away. Coasts stopped being walls: shoreSlopeMax 0.45 → 0.30, maskFalloff 0.10 → 0.16, skerryRelief 14 → 8. The kit's shards were re-exported lighter — Unity lights them again on top of the baked gradient, and at the contact-sheet values they stood two stops darker than the terrain's stone.

## 6. Key systems

### Sailing & ocean — MVP, build first
- Gerstner-wave ocean (custom, mobile-cheap). The same wave function drives water visuals, boat buoyancy/rocking, and the "roughness" input to sickness. One system, three jobs.
- Wind direction/strength; speed from sail trim vs wind angle.
- A **smoothness metric** computed from hull motion (impact accelerations, roll/pitch spikes) — this is the bridge between sailing skill and sickness rate, and the number we tune until sailing feels fair and fun.

### Seasickness — MVP
- Per-crew-member meter, **0 → 100 and monotonic at sea**. Rate = base (time at sea) + roughness² (from smoothness metric) − modifiers (traits, cook, doctor, ship comfort upgrades).
- **Puking does not relieve it.** It is the tax: past the threshold they leave their post for the rail, and the frequency and length of episodes climb with the meter until they never come back from it. "A broken crew stops working" is therefore a duty cycle, not a rule at 100%.
- **Land is the only cure.** Shore leave drains the meter to zero at a rate, so a real harvest fully settles a crew and a touch-and-go costs you the time it takes. This makes sickness a per-*leg* clock and islands genuine sanctuaries.
- Visible stages on the body, not bars-first: green tint → queasy sway → walking off to the rail → slumped over it, grey and done.
- **Potions:** settle (reset a crew member's meter) and endure (slow accumulation for a stretch). Bought/brewed at home; limited cargo slots.

### Crew & stations — MVP-lite (one crew member), full later
- Stations: helm (player-directed), sails, lookout, cannons (later), below-deck (cook/doctor, later).
- Crew as data from day one: ScriptableObject per villager — name, role, traits, sickness stats — so the roster layer slots in without rework.
- **Roster (post-MVP):** named persistent villagers; recruit from islands; breeding with heritable traits (Cult of the Lamb-style); sea legs grow with voyages; death/retirement. Example traits: Iron Stomach, Weak-Kneed, Fast Trimmer.

### Islands — outposts you govern by hand (rewritten 2026-09-20)
_The "MVP simple" timer this section used to describe was overtaken on 2026-09-13. The working spec, the trap log and every measured number live in `docs/PLAN-island-outposts.md`; this is the shape of it._

**Islands make it, the sea risks it, the ship spends it, and the ship reaches further.**

1. **Anchor.** The camera lifts to a god's-eye view of the island and the player's **Hand** appears. On islands — and only on islands — the game plays like *Black & White 2*: grab the land and pull it, orbit about what is under the cursor, dive toward it, fling, fly to a spot.
2. **Govern by hand.** Pick a crewman off the deck and put him down: on a tree to cut timber, on a blueprint to build it, on a sawmill to be its sawyer, on the ship to come back aboard. Buildings are sited as ghosts. **The Hand gives orders and never produces** — a drop writes the camp's ledger row and that is all it does.
3. **Leave them working.** An outpost is a ledger that ticks on game time whether or not anybody is looking (**D2: watching must not beat leaving**); the people on screen are its puppets. A hand ashore is an empty berth at sea. A camp's stores have a ceiling and **only building raises it**, so absentee income is bounded without a timer.
4. **Return and load.** A camp **never ships its goods back by itself**. The hold is the bottleneck, deck cargo is the greed, and the sea is where the value is at stake.
5. **Spend it ashore.** **Ship-ladder rungs are priced in made goods** — timber first, then boards (a sawmill on a wooded island), tools (a smith on an ore island), spice (far out). A bigger ship reaches further rings, further rings hold new kinds, new kinds buy the next rungs. **Village growth** (storage, recruits, the settlement's appetite) is the second sink and comes after the ship.
6. **Upkeep.** Food is local (berries subsist, wheat feeds a settlement — an island tells you from the air how many it can hold), huts cap supported hands, the campfire's brightness is the provisions gauge, neglected hands get angry, and the camp's **influence** — how far from it the Hand can reach — grows with what is built.
7. **Teeth, then the portfolio.** Raiders find rich unwatched camps; a lost camp must read as the player's mistake. The minimap becomes the dashboard of every ledger, and a save carries them.

Build order: **1** the god's-eye island (camera, Hand, living villagers) · **2** the sink (rung prices, camp → hold → home) · **3** upkeep and influence · **4** raiders and the watchtower · **5** portfolio and save.

### Village / base — post-MVP (menu-first)
Buildings as upgrade cards before they're 3D: housing (crew capacity), blacksmith (ship parts), hospital (heal sickness aftereffects, unlock doctor), farmland (food → cook effectiveness), apothecary (potions). Village becomes a walkable/visible scene only after the loop is proven.

### Ship upgrades — post-MVP
Modular parts on the ship prefab (hull, sails, rudder, comfort fittings, cargo hold). Player-provided modular 3D assets (hull, mast/sail, cannons, rudder) are the basis of the ship.

## 7. MVP milestone list
Each milestone is a committable, testable slice. Sailing first; work backward.

1. **Boat on water** — greybox portrait scene: Gerstner ocean, buoyant hull, one-thumb rudder drag, constant wind. *Exit test: steering around swells is fun with zero art.*
2. **Wind & trim** — speed from wind angle + trim order; smoothness metric computed and visualized (debug).
3. **Sickness on one crew member** — meter driven by smoothness + time; green tint + puke-at-railing behavior; potion as a tap order.
4. **The voyage loop** — depart dock → reach island anchor → auto-gather → sail back → loot tally screen.
5. ~~**Mutiny & escalation**~~ — built 2026-08-17, **removed 2026-08-19**. Replaced by crew-as-labour: sickness costs you the ship's capability instead of triggering a scripted turn-back.
6. **Crew of three** — stations, orders, per-member sickness; traits as data.
7. **Camp screen economy** — menu-based village: spend loot on 2–3 buildings + a ship upgrade + potions that visibly change the next voyage.
8. **On-device build** — real phone, portrait, one-handed input polish pass. (Earlier if feel is in doubt — feel can only be judged on the phone.)

## 8. Out of scope (for now)
- Combat: enemy ships, sea monsters. Ship design leaves room for cannons; nothing is built until sailing is fun.
- Walkable 3D village.
- Breeding/genetics depth, multi-ship fleets, weather systems beyond wind, narrative/quests, monetization.

- 2026-08-30 — **Erosion: fBm that carves valleys instead of stacking blobs.**
  - The thing no knob could fix. Each octave is damped by how steep the COARSER ones already are — `n / (1 + erosion·|grad|²)` — so fine detail survives on flats and shoulders and is suppressed on steep faces, which is what erosion does to roughness: material will not stay on a slope. What comes out is spurs with smooth flanks running down to hollows between them. Plain fBm adds smaller blobs to bigger ones and ridged noise makes crests without ever making a drainage; neither can produce this.
  - **It is an approximation, not a simulation.** Real hydraulic erosion is a stateful sweep over a finite grid; this height function is a pure function of world position running in Burst on streamed chunks. There is no water and nothing moves. What it reproduces is the statistical signature.
  - **`SimplexD` — simplex with its analytic derivative**, exact rather than a finite difference: within one simplex cell the corner offsets are the sample point minus a constant, so the derivative is the sum of the three corners' own and no chain rule through the skew is needed. **Checked against a central difference before anything was built on it: mean relative error 4.1e-3, worst 9.9e-3.** A wrong derivative does not error — it damps by the wrong thing and produces a plausible field nothing downstream would ever disagree with.
  - **`erosion = 0` reproduces `Fbm` term for term** (worst difference 1.8e-7 over 4000 samples), and that is what makes the coast safe *by construction rather than by tuning*: the erosion is faded by `interior`, the shoreline sits at mask ≈ 0.23 which is below `massifMaskStart` 0.35, so `interior` is exactly 0 there and the field is bit-for-bit plain fBm. Every beach slope and coastline number this project has measured is untouched. It also means ONE noise evaluation, not two and a lerp.
  - **The distribution had to be remapped, and the project has been burned by this before.** Measured over 60k samples: erosion leaves the mean alone (0.4995 against Fbm01's 0.4998 — unlike the ridged field, which shifted it a quarter of the range and flattened every island) but takes **13% off the spread**, 0.1604 → 0.1396, saturating by erosion 0.5. Unremapped that is 13 % less relief everywhere and it would read as the profile curve being wrong.
  - **Measured across the world, nothing regressed:** walkable median 68.5 % → **72.8 %** (erosion smooths flanks and concentrates the steep, which is the right direction for a game about walking on these), min walkable 40.4 → 41.3, `corr(r_eff, peak)` 0.78 → **0.79**, islands and size distribution unchanged.
  - **The cost, stated plainly:** it shaves the summits. Max peak/r_eff **0.82 → 0.60** and the home island's peak went 111 m → 80 m. That is physically what erosion does, and it is a real loss of drama — compensate on the relief knob if wanted. Median peak/r_eff held at 0.30.
  - **Two things NOT measured.** Perf: `SimplexD` is roughly 1.5× `Simplex` and this is the hottest function in the terrain, on a mobile target — `TerrainPerfProbe` is a MonoBehaviour inside an `Editor` folder so it cannot be attached and could not be run (the same trap `IslandLook` carries a comment about). And the visual: **`BigLook` targets the tallest island but `IslandLook` reframes on the one nearest the ship**, so every shot this session photographed a 60 m islet instead of the 249 m island that was aimed at.

- 2026-08-30 — **Rock that protrudes, and the variety problem turns out to be the opposite of what it looked like.**

  - **The near water rendered BLACK.** The storm sky pass mixed sky colour in on fresnel alone, which vanishes at normal incidence — fine when every wave was a grazing plane, useless when a steep sea shows the camera its faces. Added `_SkySoft`, an angle-independent scatter term that grows with the storm, lifted `_StormDeep`, and raised the storm ambient floor 0.19 → 0.28.
  - **A material asset snapshots a shader property's default when the property is created, and changing that default later does NOT reach it.** Verified by reading it back at runtime: `_StormDeep` still returned (0.020, 0.045, 0.055) long after the shader said (0.058, 0.086, 0.098). The `.mat` text did not even list the property. This is the scene serialization trap wearing a different hat; `SetupStormSky` now pushes the ocean palette explicitly.
  - **The decoration was all built for a calm sea, and a big one exposed it.** `SeaStreaks` and `GustVisuals` place untextured `PrimitiveType.Quad`s pinned to `Quaternion.Euler(90,0,0)` — dead horizontal. On a 40m face they cut straight through the surface and showed as grey plates. Both now take the wave normal by central difference and lie ON the surface, and use the shared soft blob. `SpeedJuice`'s foam was deliberately opaque and untextured so it would "catch the sun and read as thrown water with mass" — true under a bright sky, and wet cardboard under a storm lid at a third sun. It is alpha-clipped to a disc now.
  - **`SpeedJuice`'s impact thresholds were absolutes** (`beamImpactThreshold` 1.5 m/s²), so in the new sea every frame was a hard knock and the bursts became the weather. They now scale with `SeaSeverity01` — the same lesson the spindrift crest threshold taught, which is that a threshold into the sea has to be relative to the sea.
  - `OceanRenderer`'s mesh bounds were 80m tall. Displacement happens in the vertex shader, so Unity culls against those bounds and never sees where the surface actually went — the whole ocean would have popped out of frame. Raised to 600m; bounds are free.

- 2026-08-20 — **The sea became a noise field instead of a row of waves.**
  - Feedback: *"the top of the waves are still very sharp and very unison ... above all, the larger a wave, the wider it needs to be ... more like a noise map than a row of waves going by."* Three separate complaints, and all three measured true before anything changed.
  - **Row-ness**, a new metric: mean surface slope walking ACROSS the dominant direction divided by the same walking ALONG it. A pure wave train is flat across itself, so 1.0 is an isotropic field and 0 is corrugated iron. **Measured before: 0.55 on the home shelf, 0.35 at 600m west, 0.76 inside the storm.** The six storm cross-trains had fixed the storm and left everywhere else as rows.
  - **Cause, exactly as the old code comment admitted:** directional spread was `peakL / wavelength`, which pinned the LONG waves — the ones that carry the sea's shape — to about ±9° of the wind. The four longest base waves measured −1°, 4°, 11° and −1°. **There was no longer any reason for it:** wind stopped being physics in `3fd9309` and nothing in the gameplay reads the spectrum's directions. The arc opened right out (±80° for the longest, ±125° for the shortest).
  - **"The larger a wave, the wider it needs to be" is one identity in Gerstner:** amplitude = steepness × wavelength / 2π. So amplitude now rises in proportion to wavelength (`ampPower` 1), which makes every wave in the set equally steep and means a tall crest can only ever be a long one. The old spectrum did the opposite — it weighted steepness by a log-normal energy curve around a 45m peak, which decoupled amplitude from wavelength: **the tallest base wave was 71m and the 170m wave was the smallest in the set at 0.17m.** `amp/L` spanned 17× across the spectrum; it now spans 1.5×.
  - **`totalSteepness` was replaced by `baseAmplitude` in metres**, because it conflated two things that have to be separated: how TALL the sea is and how SHARP it is. Same for `stormSteepness` → `stormAmplitude`. That also made size independent of the seed — changing the number of random draws in the base loop had silently shrunk the storm from 12.7m of amplitude to 9.9m.
  - **Sharpness got its own dial.** Gerstner's cusped crest comes from the HORIZONTAL part of the displacement pinching the tops together, so a `choppiness` factor (0.72) scales only that. Crests round off at unchanged wave height, and it buys headroom under the self-intersection limit. Applied in four places that must agree: `Displace`, `JacobianAt`, and the shader's `WaveDisplacement` and `BreakingAmount`. **Crest skew 0.34 → 0.06.**
  - **Directions are stratified in mirrored pairs, biggest wave first.** Covering the strata is not enough on its own: amplitude now rises with wavelength, so a handful of waves decide everything, and a plain shuffle that put three of the four longest on one side just rebuilt rows on a new bearing — measured 1.88 that way round, as directional as the 0.55 it replaced, only rotated. Pairing the biggest waves into mirrored strata makes the dominant components cancel each other's bearing by construction.
  - **The storm trains stopped being *cross* trains.** Their whole justification was crossing a base spectrum that ran in rows; once it did not, six big waves all aimed 58–124° off the wind simply became the new dominant axis (row-ness 1.87 in the storm against 1.09 on the shelf, from the same base spectrum plus those). They now spread across the fan like everything else and cross each other by covering it.
  - **Result: row-ness 1.07 on the home shelf, 1.07 at 600m west, 1.07 in the full storm.** The agreement across three places with wildly different wave heights is the cross-check — it is now a property of the spectrum rather than of where you stand.
  - **Two instrument bugs found and fixed before any tuning was done on their output.** Row-ness walked a single 400m line, which is one realisation of a 20-wave field and not an estimate of it: it reported 2.13 on the home shelf against 0.99 at 600m west from very nearly the same spectrum. It now averages 32 parallel lines. And the skew grid sampled 400m at 48×48 — **8.3m spacing, which cannot resolve a 10m wave** — while using `SampleHeightFast`, whose one-iteration error is signed differently at crests than troughs. It was reporting *negative* skew, a sea with sharper troughs than crests, which Gerstner cannot produce. Now 160×160 over 300m using the 3-iteration `SampleHeight`.
  - **The cost, measured honestly.** A spread sea does not stack its peaks the way a marching one does. With the sea state **pinned** (the probe now does this — SeaState01 drifts 0.14–1.15 over minutes and scales every amplitude, and an unpinned run produced 23.5m and 8.4m from the same build), heave at 1600m west is **15.2m against the old 23.5m, pitch 16° → 9°, and roll 10° → 18°.** She pitches less and rolls considerably more, which is what a confused sea does to a hull. Amplitude was deliberately left alone so that only the shape changed; `baseAmplitude` and `stormAmplitude` are single metre values if the size wants raising.
  - `StormSpray`'s crest threshold needed no retuning at all — being a fraction of the sea's own measured spread, it re-calibrated itself to the new spectrum (mean −0.9m, crest 6.6m, threshold 2.3m).

- 2026-08-20 — **Storm sky and light: the picture catches up with the simulation.**
  - The sea had been mountainous for two commits and still looked like a nice day with unusually large water. A storm is mostly a **lighting state**, so one number now drives all of it: `SkyDirector.Storminess01`, taken from `WaveField.StormAmount01` at the ship and lifted by a swell front standing over you, eased in over a few seconds. Sky dome, sun, ambient, fog, the ocean's own palette, the spray and the camera framing all read that one value, so they cannot disagree with each other.
  - **Procedural sky (`SeaSick/Sky`), not an imported cubemap.** A gradient dome with a sun disc, a cloud deck projected onto a plane overhead (so it compresses into a bank at the skyline the way real cloud does), and ragged low scud tearing past three times faster once it is genuinely blowing. Built now rather than at milestone 3 because the water has to match the sky — a static cubemap would leave the sea the same blue under a black lid. `TimeOfDay01` slots into the same structure later.
  - **Measured, home vs 1500m west:** storminess 0.04 → 1.00, overcast 0.14 → 0.90, scud 0.93, sun 1.12 → 0.43, fog 577–1454m → **128–547m**. Visibility collapsing is most of the effect, and it is the same lever that will make night bite.
  - **Ambient is Trilight, driven by hand.** Ambient from a custom procedural skybox needs `DynamicGI.UpdateEnvironment` every frame — milliseconds on a phone. Three colours set directly cost nothing and are exact.
  - **The ocean grades by the per-vertex storm term, not by swapping material colours**, so it stays *positional*: from the home shelf you can see the dark water lying out west. Grazing angles also mix in `_SS_SkyHorizon`, so the sea can never stay blue under a black sky. Calm foam is cyan-white, which read as a lit ring around the hull under a storm — storm foam goes neutral.
  - **The camera was the real culprit, and it was not the tilt.** The plan blamed "19m up / 41° down"; the rig was actually at 22°, and the fault was that `shipFlat` forced y to 0 — **the camera was pinned to mean sea level and never followed the ship vertically at all**. In a storm that throws her 23.5m, the horizon stayed nailed and the *boat* bobbed through the frame, which is exactly backwards. It now low-passes the ship's height (rides the swell, ignores the chop) and drops 9m closer to the water as the sea builds.
  - **Measured, same spot, back to back:** ship-in-frame spread **0.0218 → 0.0126 of screen height per metre of heave (42% steadier)**, and the lens sits **8.2m above her instead of 18.1m**. *Normalising by heave was necessary* — the sea grew 3× between the first pair of windows and made the raw spreads uncomparable. The same normalised figures came back from a 90-sample run at 10fps and a 1664-sample run at ~185fps, which is a useful check that the metric is frame-rate independent.
  - **Heave is added AFTER the framing lerp, not folded into its target.** Through the lerp it becomes a second low pass stacked on the one that produced it and the rig lags the very swell it is meant to ride: that alone was the difference between 26% and 42%.
  - **Spindrift is emitted at measured crests**, with the threshold set as a *fraction of the sea's own spread* tracked across frames — no wave-spectrum API to keep in step with, and it self-calibrates to any sea state and region scale.
  - **Three bugs, all found by measuring rather than looking:**
    - The first crest threshold was an absolute 6.5m above the local mean and produced **exactly one spindrift particle alive in a full storm** — 6.5m was very nearly the ceiling of a sea that turned out to be heaving 12.4m peak to peak, not the 23.5m the storm reaches at its worst. *Tuning values are balanced against each other, never absolute.*
    - The emission budget **banked unspent allowance without a ceiling**. Sustained output was correct at 110/s, but the accumulator reached 178 particles of credit during a quiet stretch and dumped it the moment the crests returned — measured at **681/s against a 110/s budget**, and frame-rate dependent (the dump rate is `samplesPerFrame × fps`) which would have behaved differently on a phone. Unspent budget must evaporate.
    - Untextured particle quads have a hard rim: the first storm shot was full of grey **rectangles** sliding over the sea. Same mistake the bow foam made before it was turned solid. Both systems now use a soft radial blob generated in code, and spindrift is narrow and stretched along its own velocity — the first pass was 3m wide and read as cotton balls resting on the water.
  - **`SetupStormSky` resets components to their C# defaults on every run** (copying the serialised values off a freshly constructed instance). This is the fourth time the serialization trap has cost time; re-running the setup is now the fix rather than hand-editing the `.unity` file.

- 2026-08-20 — **Mountain seas built.**
  - A wall of water ~96m tall and 30m thick, running across the western deep. **Not a wave in the wave field**: Gerstner self-intersects long before that steepness, and at five times the ship's length tall and narrower than it is high this is a moving cliff, not water you ride. It is an obstacle with a procedural low-poly mesh — which is precisely what lets it have rules of its own.
  - **It mauls, it never kills.** Everything scales off one number: `beam = sin(angle between the bow and the oncoming wall)`. Bow-on *or* stern-on is survivable — what ruins you is presenting the whole length of the hull. Measured across four angles, all survived: bilge 0.16 → 0.58, way scrubbed to 6.1 → 3.4 m/s, and **deck cargo torn off only when caught across it** (20 held → 10 at 60°, → 8 at 90°).
  - **Roams rather than ambushes.** `MountainSeaDirector` launches them only in the western sector beyond 700m from home, on a heading that *crosses* the player rather than chasing, from 900m out — far enough that the silhouette is on the horizon well before it arrives. Being caught is a watch you failed to keep, not something the game did to you.
  - **The warning is an instrument, not a banner:** a plate under the compass with the countdown and *"she's lying across it"* / *"bow into it"*, plus a red pip on the compass for its bearing. It never says *turn left* — reading the angle is the skill.
  - **`HullIntegrity.TakeShot`'s `amount` is a MULTIPLIER on `damagePerShot`, not a fraction.** Passing 0.30 there costs ~2.5% hull, not 30% — which is why the first run showed a mountain sea scratching the paint (hull 0.97–0.99 at every angle). Added `Batter(point, fraction)` for anything that isn't a cannonball.
  - **Roll is a damped spring**, so sampling it a second after impact reads it already settled. But following the peak showed the real problem: **`AddRecoilRoll` cannot lay a ship over.** Its spring peaks around 0.27s and the rotation `Slerp` (`angularResponse` 2.5) has a ~0.4s time constant, so it eats the peak before the transform ever sees it — and the target is clamped to ±26° regardless. Measured: a mountain sea managed **7°**, the same reason a full broadside only heels her ~5°.
  - **Added `ShipMotor.Knockdown(degrees, seconds)`**, applied *after* the roll clamp and decaying along a sine so she goes over hard and rights herself gradually. Allowed out to ±78°, so beam-on genuinely puts the rail under. `AddRecoilRoll` stays what it is — a nudge for gunfire, not a knockdown.

- 2026-08-19 — **Wind removed as physics; the sea itself became the only thing a heading argues with.**
  - Reported as: wind *"punishes the player for wanting to explore… basically impossible to go in different directions without getting hard stuck."*
  - **The polar was not the culprit** — its floor was already 0.55, so dead upwind was slow, never a wall. The real problem was **multiplicative stacking**: `polar × wind strength × cargo × hull damage` put an upwind leg in light air with a full hold at roughly a quarter of top speed, while the UI shouted `IN IRONS`, painted a red no-go band on the nav tape and turned the wind arrow red — **the game told the player they were stuck at the exact moment they were merely slow.** Fourth instance of the standing lesson that Kevin's feel complaints are rarely the system he names; this time the honest answer was still to remove it, because he'd hit it twice across sessions.

- 2026-08-19 — **Sickness reworked: the meter only goes up, and mutiny is gone.**
  - **The meter is monotonic at sea.** 0 → 100, and nothing afloat brings it down. Puking used to shave 0.25 off it, which made the whole system a sawtooth: letting the crew heave literally bought off the mutiny, so the "clock" could be paid down with the thing that was supposed to be its symptom.
  - **Puking is now the tax, not the relief.** Past ~80% (offset per person by `ironStomach` plus a name-seeded jitter, so five people don't hit the rail on the same frame) they leave their post for the rail. Episodes lengthen 3.5s → 9s and close up 30s → 6s as the meter climbs, until at 100% they never come back — `State.Broken`, slumped over the rail, grey. **"A broken crew stops working" is a duty cycle, not a rule at 100%**, so there is no cliff anywhere on the curve.
  - **Mutiny deleted** (`MutinyController`, `Anger01`, `SailCap`, `VoyageManager.DitchCargo`). It was the *only* wire from sickness to the ship — cannons fired straight off player input and sails were set directly by the player — so removing it meant building the layer that should have been there.

    - **Guns are worked by named people.** One hand per gun in crew order (Bo→port fore, Mara→port aft, Pip→stbd fore, Tam→stbd aft; Ola is the spare/sail hand). `Cannon` reload is now a serviced countdown (`ReloadScale`, `Manned`) rather than an absolute `readyAt`, so a gunner at the rail leaves a half-rammed charge sitting there. `CannonBattery.PostGunCrews` stands each gunner at their own gun, **derived from the live gun transforms** rather than baked into the scene, so the deck can't drift out of sync with the battery. - 2026-08-19 — **Combat camera: widen, never rotate.**
  - **The lean-toward-the-broadside camera was a mistake, and a specific one.** SeaSick steers by dragging on screen, which makes the camera *the control frame*: the player's sense of "left" is defined by where it points. Every degree of combat yaw re-maps the thumb mid-fight, exactly when a precise heading is needed to keep a side bearing. It was not visual noise — it was rotating the controls while they were in use. Triggering it **per shot** compounded it: a rolling engagement became a continuous series of transitions.
  - **How ship games actually solve this.** The common patterns are (1) pull back and rise so both beams are visible, ship stays centred; (2) frame both combatants; (3) a *player-initiated* aim mode that gives up the steering reference only when the player chooses (World of Warships) — a bad fit here, since one thumb in portrait has no spare input and cannot afford to lose the helm; (4) don't move the camera at all, and make the shot readable instead (tracers, persistent splashes). The through-line: **heading stays stable on screen, because steering is the primary skill.** Aiming is served by information, not camera motion.
  - **Chosen: widen only.** While engaged, the camera goes 12m further astern, 5.5m higher, and looks 5m less far ahead — no yaw term anywhere, so the mapping between drag and rudder never changes. Tilt lands at ~24.8°, deliberately short of the 30° where the horizon leaves frame. Driven by **being engaged** (a target within 1.25× gun range), not by firing, so a fight is one transition rather than one per volley; a broadside merely refreshes the hold so the framing outlasts the shot's flight.
  - **Tracers** on every round carry the readability the camera used to: a thin smoke trail you follow down to the water. This is what makes "don't move the camera" survivable — the information moved instead of the view.
- 2026-08-19 — **Guns you can see, a hull that answers them, and shots that break something.**
  - **Raiders carry visible guns.** Four of them, two a side, built from the *same* `Cannon` component the player uses — mesh, recoil and muzzle smoke all come free, and only the ballistics differ, since a raider lays its guns by pointing the whole ship (`Cannon.RecoilOnly()` works them for the look while `EnemyShip` does the aiming). They fire from the actual muzzles now, so a broadside comes off the rail rather than out of the hull. **Scaled 1.7×** and given lighter iron: the player reads their own guns from the deck, but a raider is read at 46m and up, where a correctly-proportioned cannon is three dark pixels. Legibility at engagement range beats accuracy of proportion. Guns are built *after* the red tint is captured so they keep their own timber and iron instead of vanishing into a red hull.
  - **The hull answers the broadside.** `ShipMotor.AddRecoilRoll` is a **damped spring** (stiffness 34, damping 3.4 → ~1.1s period, underdamped), not a lerp back to level — because a boat does not return to upright, it rolls past and comes back once or twice, and that second little roll *is* the feel. ~14°/s of kick per gun, so a two-gun broadside peaks around 5° of heel. Firing to starboard heels her to port, which is where the reaction actually throws her. Added **after** the swell clamp so a broadside is felt even when the sea already has the hull hard over, with the total clamped to ±26°. Raiders get the same treatment, so you can see them roll when they fire at you.
  - **Hits break something.** `Impact.Burst` on every shot that bites: an additive flash that collapses inward, a hard scatter of sparks, lingering smoke, and a brief point light that *fades* rather than vanishing (a light that simply switches off pops). Short on purpose — long slow explosions read as fireworks; this should read as something breaking.
- 2026-08-19 — **Raiders shoot back: combat now spends the voyage clock.**
  - **Why this and not more content.** Combat was the only system that could not cost anything, so fighting was never a decision. Arming the raiders required one new thing — incoming fire — and lit up four systems that already existed: hull damage → **−40% top speed and added wallow** → rougher ride → `SmoothnessMeter` → **sickness rises faster**; and each impact → `CrewAgent.Jolt` → **sickness and anger spike** → mutiny risk. A fight now spends the voyage rather than running beside it, which is pillar #2 finally bearing on combat.
  - **`PlayerHull`** makes the player a normal `IHittable`, so being shot at uses the exact same path as shooting — one hit test, one registry, no special case. `HullIntegrity.TakeShot` costs ~8.5% hull a hit (**~12 hits to wreck**) and jolts every crew member. Deliberately slow: the timber is not what ends a voyage, the wallow and the terrified crew are, and those compound long before the hull runs out. **`Alive` is always true** — failure stays soft, exactly as §3 says: a wrecked ship is crippled, never destroyed, and the crew turning for home is the real fail state.
  - **Raiders circle instead of charging.** A bow carries no guns, so inside `engageRange` a raider stops steering at the player and starts orbiting them at ~46m — it has to give you its side to shoot you, which is the same bargain you are making. That one change is what turns a chase into an engagement.
  - **Partial lead (0.8) plus 3.4° spread** is the whole difficulty dial. Hold a straight course and they hit you; change course and their shot goes where you were going to be. The dodge costs nothing to implement because the player is already the one deciding the heading — and it rewards the sailing, not a dodge button.
  - **Now that the player is a target, every lookup has to say who is asking.** `HitTargets.Nearest`/`SweepFirst` take an `ignore`, shots carry their firer, and the traverse, aim assist and target HUD all pass themselves — otherwise the player's guns train on their own hull. Raider-on-raider fire is allowed and left in: it makes their positioning matter.
  - **Verified end to end**, ship parked in a guard zone for 30s: hull 1.000 → 0.915 (exactly one shot), `wasShot` true, crew sickness 0.21 → 1.59 across five crew. **Engagement was sparse** — one hit in 30s, because `Block` holds a standoff that is often outside the 60m gun range and only `Chase` closes. If fights feel too quiet, raise `chaseRange` (130) before touching anything else.
- 2026-08-19 — **Hit volumes are capsules, hulls are solid, and the camera leans on a broadside.**
  - **A ship is not a ball.** Targets were a single sphere, so a 21m raider wrapped in a 5.5m sphere let shots at the bow or stern pass clean through — it read as having no hit box at all. `IHittable` now also reports `HitAxis` (half the body length, in world space) and `HitTargets.SweepFirst` tests **segment-vs-capsule** when it is non-zero, falling back to the sphere when it is not. Raiders are a 4.2m-radius capsule ~16m long; the monster is 4.2m by ~9m. Closest-approach between the two segments rather than a swept-capsule quadratic: far cheaper, and near enough to the entry point for a ball crossing a hull.
  - **Hulls are solid to each other.** Nothing did ship-vs-ship collision, so the player sailed straight through raiders and raiders slowly converged into each other on a shared patrol circle. `HullIntegrity` now treats raiders as obstacles — **ramming costs hull and stops you dead**, at 0.55× a reef's bite because timber gives where rock does not — and `EnemyShip.KeepClear` shoves off the player and off other raiders. Separation (7.5m) is deliberately less than a ship's length so two vessels can still lie alongside for a broadside; the point is to stop them merging, not to fend them off the moment it gets interesting.
  - **The camera leans to watch the fall of shot.** A broadside pushes the chase camera's **look point** out along the firing side (34m) and lifts it slightly (5.5m), held for ~1.7s — about one time of flight — then drifts back. Crucially it moves the look point, never the anchor, and **never retargets onto a shell**: cutting to the shot and back is exactly what would make this unwatchable. The ship stays in frame throughout and the horizon never rolls. Eased in at 3.2/s and out at 1.1/s, so it arrives with the shot and forgets about it gently; firing again extends the hold rather than restarting the lean, so a rolling engagement leans once instead of pumping on every volley.
- 2026-08-19 — **Raider polish: solid shores, the player's hull in red, and space as the dock key.**
  - **Raiders were clipping through islands.** Steering could never fix this on its own: a raider makes 17 m/s and turns at 26°/s, so its turning circle is ~38m while an island can be 200m across — it physically cannot always turn in time. Three changes: the lookahead now **scales with speed** (a fixed 70m is fine at steerage way and far too short at full speed), patrol clearance 55 → 78m, and — the actual fix — **`KeepClear()` makes the shoreline solid**, shoving the hull back out to `RadiusToward + margin` and bleeding 45% of its way, exactly as `HullIntegrity` already does for the player. Avoidance is now the nicety and the shove is the guarantee.
  - **Raiders wear the player's ship, tinted red.** `BuildFromPlayer()` clones the live `PlayerShip`'s three visual children (`Hull`, `MastPivot`, `RudderPivot`) — crew are separate children and are deliberately left behind — strips every MonoBehaviour and collider off the copy, and tints via **`MaterialPropertyBlock` rather than material instances**, so raiders still batch with the player's hull. Cloned from the scene rather than a prefab so there is no second asset to keep in sync: a raider is always whatever the ship currently is. Built on the first `Update`, not in `Awake`, because they spawn from `ArchipelagoGenerator.Awake` and script order is not guaranteed. The primitive hull survives as a fallback.

- 2026-08-19 — **Raiders: enemy ships that guard islands.**
  - **They sail, they don't drive.** `EnemyShip` takes its speed off the same polar the player does (`ShipMotor.SailPolar`, `NoGoDegrees`, `BestUpwindAngle`), so a raider cannot point straight upwind either — it beats, and it slows in irons exactly as you do. This matters more than any chase logic: it means a **wind advantage is a real advantage**, and running downwind is a genuine escape rather than an animation. Combat inherits the sailing pillar instead of sitting beside it.
  - **Pathfinding is steering, not a graph.** The sea is open water with a handful of round obstacles that already publish their own radii (`Island.RadiusToward`, `Reef.Radius`), so raiders use a lookahead plus a lateral shove along the tangent. Cheaper than a grid and more ship-like: a raider bears away from a shoal rather than following waypoints around it, and it never stops to think — a ship that halts reads as broken.
  - **Duties: Patrol → Block → Chase → Return.** Patrol steers to a point *further round* the orbit circle than the raider currently is, which gives a smooth sweep instead of the in-out weave you get from chasing the nearest point on the circle. **Block is the interesting one** — it puts the hull between the island and the intruder rather than charging, because that is what guarding actually means. Chase leads the player's velocity. Stand-down range (340m) is deliberately longer than alert range (260m) so a raider doesn't dither while the player circles at the edge.
  - **Only islands worth taking get guarded** — home and bare shelter rocks are skipped, since posting a guard on empty rock would just be a toll on safe harbour. [superseded 2026-09-22: no home island — see the innermost raider-free ring, `WorldSettings.raiderFreeRing`] Patrol direction alternates so two raiders on one island sweep opposite ways.
  - **They cost almost nothing to add** because `IHittable`/`HitTargets` was already the seam: the player's guns hit them, the aim assist and traverse already target them, and they take damage, flash, splinter and sink with no changes to the gunnery at all. `IHittable` gained `Health01`/`HitPoints`/`DamageTaken` so `TargetHUD` draws any target rather than only monsters, and raiders show their current duty above the health bar — knowing a guard has switched to coming for you is worth more than its hit points.
  - Minimap: raiders are red blips with a faint ring showing the water each one patrols; the patrol is the information, not the ship's momentary position.
  - **Unarmed on purpose.** They intercept and block but carry no guns yet, so there is still no fight — only a blockade. Arming them means the player needs damage coming back (hull, crew panic, sinking), which is the next increment and a bigger one than it looks.
- 2026-08-19 — **Why hitting was hard: the guns were riding the roll.**
  - **The cause, measured directly.** The guns are rigid children of the hull and point over the beam, so roll maps 1:1 into elevation. Under sail the barrel swung between **1.0° and 15.6°** (hull roll −6.6°…+7.9°), which lands the shot anywhere from **29.5m to 102.3m**. Against a target 8.4m wide that is not difficulty, it is a lottery — and nothing on screen tells the player where the hull is in its roll cycle. This measurement needs no hit-rate test and is the one solid finding of the session.
  - **Roll stabilisation** (`Cannon.rollStabilisation`, default 0.75): the barrel is laid against the horizon rather than the deck. Diegetically this is just what a gun crew does. Held below 1 on purpose so a heavy sea still costs accuracy as a *gradient*, matching how wind and sickness already work. Verified directly: elevation at the moment of firing goes from a 1.0–15.6° spread to sitting on the nominal 9°.
  - **Gun traverse** (`maxTraverseDeg` 18°, `trainWithinDeg` 60°): the crew train the guns onto the nearest beast, widening "the side bears" from a ~10° knife-edge into a zone. The ball still flies exactly where the barrel points — no projectile is bent. **Traverse must rotate about world up, not the gun's own up**: its own up is the mast, which leans with the roll, so swinging the gun sideways around a leaning axis also swings it in elevation. Measured at −10.4° before the fix, i.e. broadsides fired into the sea.
  - **Aim assist** (`aimAssistWindow` 8m, `aimAssistCap` 2.5m): the muzzle velocity is nudged once, at firing, toward the launch angle that passes through the target; the shot stays a clean parabola. The correction is capped in **metres at the target's range**, so a long shot bends no further than a short one. First version measured the miss where the ball reached the *water*, which is wrong twice over — the beast sits above the waterline and the shot passes through it on the way down — so a good shot read as a 20m miss and the assist never engaged at all.
  - **`velocityInheritance` still 0.** Unchanged and still undecided.
  - **Hit-rate A/B testing did not work and its results are discarded.** Identical baseline code scored 83%, 50% and 17% across runs. Sea state varies over a session (`WaveField`/`SwellDirector`), so separate play sessions are not comparable, and several runs landed in near-calm water where the barrel sat at a constant 9° and stabilisation had nothing to fix. **Any future gunnery A/B must pin sea state**, not just the ship. Harness lessons that cost real time: editing anything under `Assets/` mid-run triggers a domain reload that silently kills the running coroutine; an "anchored" ship still makes ~11 m/s and is 30m from where it was put within 3 seconds; and guns slew at 55°/s, so firing immediately after repositioning catches them mid-swing.

It does ride the swell, so the guns still cope with a target that rises and falls. - 2026-08-18 — **Hull works the sea; waves break against it.**
  - **Six float points instead of three.** With a bow and two stern points the hull was effectively rigid; sampling down the length (stem, fore pair, aft pair, transom) and fitting mean height / fore-aft / port-starboard lets the **bow ride up while midships is still in a trough**. Roll and pitch are clamped (±20° / ±16°) — the raw fit reached nearly 30° beam-on to a swell, which read as capsizing rather than working the sea.

- 2026-08-18 — **Living water: persistent wake, surface detail, shader-side foam.**
  - **The core problem was that the wake was an analytic shape bolted to the hull** — evaluated relative to the ship's *current* position and heading, so it rotated with the ship instead of being left behind. That's the tell that made the boat read as sliding over a surface.
  - **`WakeTexture`**: a 512² world-anchored RGBA buffer covering 420m around the ship. Each frame it scrolls to compensate for movement (snapped to texel boundaries so it never crawls), decays, and stamps whatever is touching the water. R = displacement, G = foam. The ocean shader samples it for both. **The wake is now history** — it stays where you sailed and curves through your turns. `WakeTexture.Splash()` is the public hook, so **cannonball splashes are essentially free** when combat lands.

- 2026-08-18 — **The sea moves the ship.** Waves previously only pushed *along the hull*, so a beam-on swell could not move the boat at all and sitting still in a storm did nothing.
  - Wave force is now a **2D world vector** from the surface gradient (sampled in x and z), not a scalar along the bow. It shoves the ship whichever way it faces. Along-hull component passes in full, across-hull is scaled by `lateralWaveScale` (0.4) because a hull resists a beam sea, then capped by `maxWaveAccel`.
  - **Stokes drift is modelled as the water mass moving, not a force.** Key insight after two failed attempts: adding drift to `velocity` let keel grip and sail drag eat it (a keel resists moving *through* water, not being carried *with* it), and adding it as a constant *acceleration* accumulated without limit.
